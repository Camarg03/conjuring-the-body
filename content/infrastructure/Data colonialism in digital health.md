# Data colonialism in digital health

## Source

Key references:

- Ferryman, K. (2021). *The dangers of data colonialism in precision public health*. Global Policy, 12(S6), 90–92. https://doi.org/10.1111/1758-5899.12953
- Qato, D. M. (2022). *Reflections on “decolonizing” big data in global health*. Annals of Global Health, 88(1), Article 56. https://doi.org/10.5334/aogh.3709
- Sekalala, S., & Chatikobo, T. (2024). *Colonialism in the new digital health agenda*. BMJ Global Health, 9(2), e014131. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjgh-2023-014131
- The Lancet Digital Health. (2023). *Decolonising health data*. The Lancet Digital Health, 5(8), e477. https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(23)00132-2

## Extraction after care

Digital health is often presented as a promise: better prediction, wider access, faster diagnosis, and more efficient systems. But medical data infrastructures can also reproduce extractive relations. Bodies are scanned, measured, recorded, and transformed into data that may circulate far from the communities, institutions, and contexts that produced them.

Data colonialism names this problem. It asks who produces health data, who stores it, who has the infrastructure to process it, who benefits from the models built from it, and who remains subject to decisions made elsewhere.

For *Conjuring the Body*, this framework connects contemporary medical AI to the longer colonial history of anatomical extraction and visual control.

## Decontextualized health data

Ferryman's work on data colonialism in precision public health is useful because it frames health data extraction as a problem of context and power. Data can be collected from populations and then used in systems that do not return benefits to those same populations, or that interpret them through categories imposed from outside.

This is especially important when medical images are treated as universally usable data. A scan may appear technically standard because it follows a format like DICOM, but the body it records belongs to a specific social, geographic, economic, and historical context.

When that context is stripped away, the image becomes easier to circulate. It also becomes easier to misread.

## Sovereignty and governance

Qato's reflection on decolonizing big data in global health brings the problem of sovereignty into the center of health data ethics. The question is not only whether data can be anonymized or shared safely. The question is who has the right to define the terms of collection, storage, use, refusal, and benefit.

The Lancet Digital Health editorial on decolonising health data makes this question especially explicit in relation to Indigenous data sovereignty. Health data governance cannot be added after collection as an administrative layer. It has to shape the conditions under which data is produced and used.

For this archive, sovereignty is not only a legal concept. It is a way to ask whether bodily fragments still belong to someone, somewhere, after they become files.

## The new digital health agenda

Sekalala and Chatikobo criticize the contemporary digital health agenda when it presents technological innovation as a universal solution while ignoring regulation, commercialization, local contexts, and colonial histories. Their argument is useful because it shows that digital health can reproduce older hierarchies through new infrastructures.

The language of modernization can make unequal technological systems appear inevitable. Communities may be framed as needing to catch up, while platforms, standards, storage systems, and analytics remain controlled elsewhere.

This connects directly to medical imaging AI. The ability to scan, store, label, and train on bodies depends on expensive infrastructures that are unevenly distributed across the world.

## Quantifiable care

The final note in the CBK bibliography raises an important tension: if automated decision-support systems become more central to health care, then quantifiable and computable aspects of health may receive more attention, while contextual information, patient experience, and values move into the background.

This is not only a technical limitation. It is a change in what counts as care.

A system can be powerful precisely because it can compute what has been formatted as data. But what is not easily formatted may become secondary: pain, fear, cultural meaning, family relation, spiritual understanding, distrust, memory, or forms of illness that exceed organ-based diagnosis.

## Relation to the colonial wound

*Conjuring the Body* begins from the wound produced when Western anatomical and medical visualization separates the body into visible, classifiable, extractable parts. Data colonialism extends this wound into the digital present.

The body is no longer only opened in the anatomical theater. It is opened through scans, segmented through software, stored in repositories, processed by cloud platforms, and translated into predictive models.

The colonial question returns in another form: whose body becomes material for knowledge, and who controls the knowledge produced from it?

## Why this matters for the archive

This entry should function as a conceptual bridge. It connects the historical entries on anatomy with the infrastructure entries on datasets, black boxes, cloud workflows, consent, and fairness.

It also prevents the archive from treating medical AI as only a technical problem. The issue is not simply bad data or opaque models. It is the organization of power around bodily information.

A medical dataset can be open and still unequal. A model can be accurate and still extractive. A scan can be anonymized and still belong to a history of dispossession.
